Reading Time Calculator Online

A reading time calculator estimates how long a text takes to read — the "5 min read" label you see on articles. This free tool also estimates speaking time for speeches, scripts, and presentations, using standard 200/150 words-per-minute averages. Paste any text above for an instant reading time estimate.

Key takeaways

  • Reading time = words ÷ 200 WPM; speaking time = words ÷ 150 WPM — both rounded up to whole minutes. It doubles as a words per minute calculator: divide any word count by 200 (reading) or 150 (speaking).
  • 200 WPM reflects average adult silent reading speed; 150 WPM reflects typical presentation pace.
  • Shows live word count alongside both time estimates.
  • Rounding up means short texts always show at least "1 min".

What Is a Reading Time Calculator?

A reading time calculator is a tool that estimates how long it takes to read a piece of text. It counts the words and divides by an average reading speed (words per minute), producing the familiar "X min read" label used by Medium, news sites, and blogs. A reading time estimator helps writers set expectations, helps speakers time presentations, and helps readers decide whether they have time for an article right now.

How This Reading Time Calculator Works

The math is deliberately simple and transparent:

  • Word count: your text is trimmed and split at whitespace — each token counts as one word.
  • Reading time: word count ÷ 200 words per minute (average adult silent reading speed), rounded up to the next whole minute.
  • Speaking time: word count ÷ 150 words per minute (typical speaking pace for presentations and narration), rounded up.

Results update live as you type. The Sample button loads a demo paragraph, Paste pulls from your clipboard, and Clear resets. Because of the round-up, a 201-word text shows "2 min" and anything under 200 words shows "1 min" — the estimate is conservative by design.

Worked Example

Take a 400-word article draft:

A 1,500-word essay: 1,500 ÷ 200 = 7.5 → 8 min read; spoken: 1,500 ÷ 150 = 10 → 10 min. That 8-minute figure is why "8 min read" is such a common label on medium-length articles.

How to Use the Reading Time Calculator

  1. Paste or type your text into the box (or click Sample to try the demo).
  2. Read the three cards: word count, reading time, and speaking time update live.
  3. Use reading time for article labels ("5 min read") and speaking time — this page works as a speak time calculator — for speeches, voiceovers, and video scripts.

Where Do 200 and 150 WPM Come From?

These are not arbitrary numbers:

  • 200–250 WPM is the widely cited range for average adult silent reading of general non-fiction in English. This tool uses the conservative end (200), so estimates err on the side of "takes a bit longer".
  • 130–160 WPM is the typical range for spoken English — newsreaders sit near 160, conversational speech nearer 130. The tool's 150 WPM lands in the middle, suitable for presentations and narration.

Both are averages: technical material, unfamiliar vocabulary, and second-language reading run slower; light fiction and skimming run faster. Treat the output as a planning estimate, then adjust for your audience and content difficulty. Note this is an estimator, not a reading speed test — it applies a fixed average rather than measuring your personal pace.

Reading Time vs Speaking Time: Which to Use?

Use reading time forUse speaking time for
Blog "X min read" labelsSpeech and presentation timing

E-book progress estimatesAudiobook length estimates

Rule of thumb: if eyes will read it, use reading time; if a voice will perform it, use speaking time — and rehearse once, because real delivery with pauses runs longer than the raw WPM math. Pair with the character counter when a platform caps characters, and trim overlong drafts first with the paragraph shortener.

Limitations

  • Round-up only: everything under 200 words reads "1 min" — the tool cannot show seconds.
  • Fixed speeds: you cannot adjust the WPM for fast readers, children, or technical audiences.
  • Word-based only: images, videos, code blocks, and tables in an article add real consumption time the calculator ignores.
  • Language differences: the 200/150 averages are calibrated for English; other languages read at different rates.
  • Whitespace tokenization: hyphenated words and numbers each count as one word, matching the site's word counter.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is reading time calculated?

Word count divided by 200 words per minute, rounded up to the next whole minute. A 400-word text gives exactly 2 minutes; a 450-word text gives 3 minutes (2.25 rounded up).

What is the average reading speed in WPM?

About 200–250 words per minute for average adult silent reading of general English text. This calculator uses 200 WPM — the conservative end — so estimates tend to slightly overstate rather than understate time.

How long does it take to read 1,000 words?

About 5 minutes of silent reading (1,000 ÷ 200) and about 7 minutes aloud (1,000 ÷ 150 = 6.67, rounded up).

What is a good speaking pace in words per minute?

Roughly 130–160 WPM for English. Newsreaders approach 160, conversational speech sits near 130. This tool's speak-time estimate uses 150 WPM, a solid middle for presentations and narration.

How long does it take to read a 300-page book?

It depends on words per page — roughly 250–300 for a typical paperback, so 75,000–90,000 words total. At 200 WPM that is 6–8 hours of reading time. Paste the actual text for an exact figure.

Why does everything under 200 words show '1 min'?

The calculator rounds up to whole minutes and has no seconds display, so any text from 1 to 200 words shows 1 minute. It is conservative by design.

Should I use reading time or speaking time for a video script?

Speaking time. Scripts are performed, not silently read — and add a margin for pauses, emphasis, and retakes, which the raw WPM math does not include.

Does images or video in my article affect reading time?

Yes in reality, no in this tool. The calculator is word-based only; images, embeds, and code blocks add consumption time it cannot see. For media-heavy pages, add your own allowance on top.

Is my text uploaded or stored?

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser. Nothing leaves your device.
